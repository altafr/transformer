Three ideas to know first
Concept 1 of 3 · used for attention scores

Dot product: how well do two lists match?

In shortMultiply two lists of numbers position by position, then add everything up. A big total means they point the same way.

A list of numbers is called a vector. The dot product compares two vectors of the same length: multiply them position by position, then add everything up. The total is a match score. It is large and positive when the lists agree, near zero when they are unrelated, and negative when they disagree.

ExampleA support ticket needs billing 3, technical 0, urgent 2. Agent A offers 3, 1, 2. Agent B offers 0, 4, 0. Agent A scores 3×3 + 0×1 + 2×2 = 13. Agent B scores 3×0 + 0×4 + 2×0 = 0. The ticket goes to Agent A.
a · b = a₁×b₁ + a₂×b₂ + … + aₙ×bₙ
Where it is usedIn attention, every word’s query is dot-multiplied with the key of each earlier word to score how relevant it is (decoder step 3b). At the very end, the final vector is dot-multiplied with every word’s row to score the whole vocabulary (step 7).

The same idea as arrows: drag the angle

For two arrows of length 1 the dot product is just the cosine of the angle between them: +1 when they point the same way, 0 at a right angle, −1 when they point opposite ways. Longer arrows scale the total up, which is why attention divides its scores by a size factor.

Presenter notes

Likely questions

Why multiply and add instead of just comparing the lists?
It boils two lists down to one number that is very cheap to compute. GPUs do billions of these per second, and everything in a transformer is built from them.
Does a bigger total always mean a better match?
Not quite. Longer lists or bigger numbers give bigger totals. That is why attention divides its scores by √dk, and why cosine similarity (the angle alone) is used when only direction matters.
Is this the same as matrix multiplication?
A matrix multiplication is many dot products at once: each output number is the dot product of one row with one column.
Can the total be negative?
Yes. It means the two lists point in opposite directions.

Slip-ups to avoid

  • Don’t say “the dot product is the similarity” without adding that the length of the lists also matters.
  • Both lists must be the same length.
  • Dot product gives one number. The cross product, which gives a vector, is not used here.
  • The ticket numbers are made up for the demo. In a model the numbers are learned, not chosen by hand.
Concept 2 of 3 · used for attention weights and next-word odds

Softmax: turn scores into shares of 100%

In shortSoftmax turns any list of scores into percentages that add up to 100%. The biggest score takes the biggest share.

Scores can be any size, even negative. Softmax converts them into shares that are all positive and add up to 100%. It takes two moves: make every score positive with escore, then divide each one by the total. A higher score always gets a bigger share, and gaps between scores get stretched, so the leader stands out.

ExampleAfter “The cat sat on the”, a model scores mat 3.6, sofa 3.0, floor 2.7, moon −1.0. Softmax gives mat 51%, sofa 28%, floor 21%, moon 0.5%. Add the same number to every score and the shares do not change; only the gaps matter.
softmax(sᵢ) = esᵢ / T ÷ Σ esⱼ / T    (T = temperature, 1 by default)
Where it is usedAttention uses it to turn scores into how much to listen to each word (step 3b), and the final layer uses it to turn scores into next-word probabilities (step 7). Temperature is the T in the formula.

Presenter notes

Likely questions

Why not just divide each score by the total?
Plain division breaks on negative scores (you would get negative shares) and gives a score of zero exactly 0%. The exponential makes every score positive and also stretches the gaps.
What is “e”?
A constant, about 2.718. All you need to know is that ex is always positive and grows fast.
Why does adding the same number to every score change nothing?
Every escore gets multiplied by the same factor, and that factor cancels when you divide by the total. Only the gaps between scores matter.
What does temperature do?
It divides the scores before softmax. Low temperature widens the gaps, so the top word dominates. High temperature shrinks them, so the shares even out.
Does 51% mean the model is 51% sure it is right?
It is the model’s relative preference among the options, not a guarantee of correctness. Models can be confidently wrong.

Slip-ups to avoid

  • Softmax gives a probability distribution (positive, sums to 1), not a “normal distribution”.
  • Softmax doesn’t choose a word; it only converts scores. The sampling step chooses.
  • The shares always add to 100%, even when the model is unsure. Uncertainty shows up as flat shares, not as a smaller total.
  • A score of −∞ (the attention mask) gives exactly 0%. A score of 0 does not: e0 = 1.
Concept 3 of 3 · used inside the MLP

Non-linearity: a bend in the line

In shortMultiplying and adding only gives straight lines. A bend, like an IF rule, lets stacked layers learn curves and “it depends” patterns.

Most of what a neural network does is multiply and add. Those steps are linear: the output moves in a straight line with the input, and however many you chain together, you still get one straight-line rule. A non-linearity is a small bend inserted between them, such as “if the number is negative, make it zero”. It is the same idea as an IF in a workflow. With bends in between, each layer can build on the one before.

ExampleA shipping fee is free below $50, then 5% of the amount above $50. A straight line cannot do that, because the rule changes at $50. The fee is max(0, 0.05 × (order − 50)): the “max with zero” is the bend.
Where it is usedInside the MLP of every block (decoder step 6), between its two matrices. Real models use smooth versions of the same bend called GELU or SiLU. The squash you tuned in the neuron demo was also a bend.
1 · Stacking straight steps gives one straight step

Two layers, y = 2x + 1 and then z = 3y − 2, look like two steps. But z = 6x + 1 does the same job in one. Chain a hundred straight layers and you can still replace them with one. Depth adds nothing without a bend.

2 · Add bends and the line can follow a curve

3 · The bend itself

ReLU (dashed) is the plain bend: below zero the output is 0, above zero it passes straight through. GELU (solid) is a smooth version used in GPT‑2. Modern models often use a close relative, SiLU. They all do the same job: put a bend between the matrices.

Presenter notes

Likely questions

Isn’t a neural network all just multiplying?
Multiplying and adding, plus bends. Take the bends away and the whole network collapses into a single multiplication.
Which bends do real models use?
ReLU (zero if negative), and smooth versions such as GELU and SiLU. Same idea.
Is attention non-linear too?
Yes, softmax is a non-linearity. The MLP adds an explicit bend for each word on top of that.
How many bends does a real model have?
One per neuron in every MLP, so millions to billions. Training decides where each bend sits.
Why does the shipping example need a bend?
“Free below $50, then 5% of the rest” changes slope at $50. A straight line can’t change slope.

Slip-ups to avoid

  • Don’t say “non-linear means complicated maths”. Say “the output doesn’t move in a straight line with the input”.
  • Stacked straight layers are not useless because they are shallow; they are useless because they collapse into one.
  • The wave demo is illustrative. Real networks learn where to put the bends; you don’t place them.
  • Each bend is one small neuron. “Bend” and “neuron” are not different things in the demo.

Attention Is All You Need

        
In shortA 2017 Google paper showed that a model built only on “attention” translates better than older designs and trains much faster.

        
In 2017 a team at Google (Vaswani and seven co-authors) published a new design for machine translation. Earlier systems read a sentence one word at a time. The transformer dropped that and used attention alone, letting every word look at every other word at once. It translated more accurately and trained much faster.

        
ExampleOlder systems read “The cat sat on the mat” like someone reading aloud: one word, then the next, so a 40-word sentence needed 40 steps in a row. The transformer takes in all six words at the same moment, which suits GPUs that do thousands of calculations in parallel.

Overview 4 of 4

        
One paper, three families of models

        
In shortBERT kept the reader, GPT kept the writer. Chatbots are writers: they continue your text.

        
Researchers soon found that each half is powerful on its own. Keep only the encoder and you get models that understand text. Keep only the decoder and you get models that generate text. Chat models like GPT and Claude are decoder-only: there is no separate source sentence, so there is no encoder and no cross-attention. Step 0 · before the journey

        
Where the numbers come from: training

        
In shortTraining means: guess the next word, see how wrong the guess was, nudge every number slightly, repeat billions of times.

        
Every number in the model (the embedding table, WQ, WK, WV, the MLP weights, the output layer) starts as a random number. Training shows the model real text and, at every position, asks it to guess the next word. After each guess, a method called backpropagation works out, for every single number, whether nudging it up or down would have made the right word a little more likely. Every number takes a tiny step in that direction, and the process repeats billions of times.

        
ExampleTraining text: “the cat sat on the mat”. The model sees “the cat sat on the” and must guess the next word. Untrained, it gives “mat” about 0.002% (one chance in 50,000). The loss is high, so every number shifts a little toward making “mat” more likely. After millions of similar sentences it gives “mat”, “floor” and “sofa” high probabilities.

        
loss = −log p(actual next token)    every weight w ← w − η · ∂loss/∂w

        
Key ideaNobody designs the embeddings or WQ, WK, WV by hand. They are whatever numbers made next-token prediction work best on the training text. When you use the model (steps 1 to 9), they are frozen and only read.

Step 1 of 9

        
Tokenization: text becomes numbers

        
In shortChop the text into pieces (tokens) and replace each piece with its number in a fixed list.

        
A neural network cannot read letters. The tokenizer cuts the text into tokens (whole words, pieces of words, or punctuation) and swaps each one for its ID in a fixed vocabulary. Each ID then picks out one row of the embedding table: a learned list of numbers that stands for that token’s meaning.

        
ExampleA common word such as “cat” is one token. A rare word such as “unbelievably” may be split into pieces like “un” + “believ” + “ably”. Each piece gets an ID number, like a page number in a dictionary, and that number is what the model actually receives.

        
Key ideaFrom here on, every token is a vector. This demo uses 16 numbers per token; GPT‑2 small uses 768. The table itself was learned in step 0: training kept adjusting each row until the vectors were useful for predicting the next word.

Presenter notes · Your script: Step 1
Your talk track (Step 1: text to tokens to embeddings)
First, the model can’t read text; it only works with numbers. So the input is converted in two stages.
Stage 1, tokenisation. A tokenizer splits the text into tokens. A token is a subword chunk, not a whole word. Common words are usually one token; rare words, names and numbers split into several pieces. As a rule of thumb, in English one token is about three-quarters of a word, so 100 tokens is roughly 75 words. Each token maps to an integer ID from a fixed vocabulary of tens of thousands of entries. The tokenizer is deterministic, rule-based and frozen. Think of it as a parser step in a flow: same input, same output, no learning at runtime.
Stage 2, embedding. Each token ID is then looked up in an embedding table and replaced by a vector, a list of a few thousand numbers. Think of it as a VLOOKUP from ID to a row of numbers. The key point: this table is not a separate model plugged in. It is the first layer of the network, and its values are learned together with the rest of the model during training.
So our example becomes: 4 tokens → 4 IDs → 4 vectors, each of width dmodel. That width stays constant through the whole model.

Step 1b of 9 · embeddings up close

        
Embeddings: meaning you can measure

        
In shortEvery token gets a list of numbers. Training makes similar words get similar lists; using the model just looks them up.

        
An embedding is a list of numbers that stands for a token. The whole vocabulary lives in one big embedding table: one row per token. Nobody writes those numbers. They are learned in training from how words are used, following the idea that you shall know a word by the company it keeps. Words used in similar places end up with similar numbers, so closeness in the table means closeness in meaning. When the model runs (inference), the table is frozen and simply looked up.

Your talk track (Step 3: the decoder block, overview)
Now we reach the core: the decoder block. Each block takes in our token vectors and passes out vectors of exactly the same shape, but enriched with context. Inside there are two sub-steps. Your talk track (Step 4: scoring and mixing)
To decide who matters, each token’s query is compared with every token’s key using a dot product. A high score means ‘highly relevant to me’. We then divide by the square root of the key dimension. Dot products grow as vectors get longer; without this scaling the next step, softmax, becomes extreme and learning stalls.
Softmax turns each token’s scores into weights: all positive, summing to 1, like percentages of attention. Finally, we use those weights to take a weighted average of the value vectors. The result for each token is a context vector: a blend of information from the tokens it attended to.

After attention has gathered information from other tokens, the second sub-step is the feed-forward network, or MLP. If attention is the communication step, the MLP is the computation step.
Here is how it works. It takes each token’s vector and expands it to a much wider layer, roughly two-and-a-half to four times wider; for example, 768 numbers expand to 3,072 in GPT-2, and 4096 to around 11,000 in a Llama-style model. The result is added back onto the token’s vector through the residual connection, and the block is complete.

Presenter notes · Your script: Step 9
Your talk track (Step 9: sampling)
The model has handed us a table of probabilities. It does not choose; a separate sampling step does. Importantly, none of these settings change the model. They only change how we pick from the same table.
The simplest approach is greedy decoding: always take the single most likely token. It is predictable, but for open-ended text it can become dull and repetitive.
So we usually sample: pick randomly, in proportion to the probabilities. Think of a roulette wheel where each token’s slice is its probability. Three controls shape that wheel.
Temperature reshapes the probabilities before we sample. It divides the logits ahead of softmax. A low temperature sharpens the distribution, so the top choices dominate and output becomes more consistent. A high temperature flattens it, so unlikely tokens get a real chance and output becomes more varied. As temperature approaches zero, it behaves like greedy.
Top-k keeps only the k most likely tokens, for example the top 40, throws the rest away, and rescales the survivors so they again sum to 1.
Top-p, also called nucleus sampling, keeps the smallest group of tokens whose probabilities add up to p, for example 0.9. It adapts: when the model is confident, only a few tokens survive; when it is unsure, many do.
In practice these combine: temperature first, then top-k and top-p to trim, then one random draw. For deterministic automation tasks such as extraction or classification, use a low temperature. For brainstorming or drafting, raise it.

Basics 5 of 6

        
From 13 dials to 175 billion

        
In shortLarge language models use the same parts: far more dials, and the task is “what comes next?”.

        
Large language models are built from exactly these parts: neurons that multiply, add and squash, stacked in many layers. The task changes from “good fruit?” to “what is the next word?”, and the dial count grows enormously. Training is the same loop: guess the next word in real text, measure the error, turn every dial a little. Inference is the same forward pass, repeated once per generated word.

Basics 6 of 6

        
The shape of the network is a design choice

        
In shortHow the neurons are wired is chosen by researchers to fit the data, and it keeps evolving.

        
Every network on this page uses the same parts: dials, weighted sums, squashes, and training by gradient descent. What changes is how the neurons are wired together, which is called the architecture. There is no single correct shape. Researchers design an architecture to match the structure of what the network has to learn, test it, and keep what works. New architectures are an active area of research, and many modern systems combine several.

        
ExampleIn photos, a cat in the top-left corner and a cat in the bottom-right corner should both count as “cat”. A convolutional network builds that in by sliding the same small filter over every part of the image.

        
Key ideaAn architecture is a built-in assumption about the data. If nearby pixels matter, use filters that look at small neighbourhoods. If order matters, read one step at a time. If any word can relate to any other word, let every word look at every other word. Choosing that assumption well often matters as much as having more data.

Feed-forward network (MLP)

            
Assumes: nothing about order or position; every input connects to every neuron.

            
Good for: tabular data, and as a building block inside bigger models.

            
Our fruit network · the MLP inside every transformer layer

          

          

            
            
Convolutional network (CNN)

            
Assumes: nearby pixels belong together, and a pattern means the same thing anywhere in the image. Small filters slide across it.

            
Good for: images, video, medical scans.

            
LeNet read handwritten zip codes (1989) · AlexNet won ImageNet (2012)

          

          

            
            
Recurrent network (RNN, LSTM)

            
Assumes: data arrives in order. It reads one step at a time and carries a memory forward.

            
Good for: speech, sensor streams, and text before 2017.

            
LSTM (1997) · powered Google Translate’s 2016 neural system

          

          

            
            
Transformer

            
Assumes: any token may relate to any other, however far apart. Attention lets every token look at every other token, all in parallel.

            
Good for: language, code, translation, and now images and audio too.

            
“Attention Is All You Need” (2017) · GPT, Claude, Gemini

          

          

            
            
Diffusion model

            
Assumes: an image can be made by starting from pure noise and removing a little noise at a time. A network (often a U-Net) learns each clean-up step.

            
Good for: generating images, audio and video.

            
DDPM (2020) · Stable Diffusion (2022)

          

          

            
            
Graph neural network (GNN)

            
Assumes: the data is a set of things with connections between them. Each node updates itself from its neighbours.

            
Good for: molecules, social networks, road traffic.

            
Google Maps arrival-time predictions (DeepMind, 2020)

          

        

        
These families also mix. Vision Transformers (2020) apply attention to patches of an image, and diffusion models often contain attention layers. The next tab looks closely at the architecture behind today’s language models.
